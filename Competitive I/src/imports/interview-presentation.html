// Part 1 · The Business Problem I Solved

Customers Were
Churning Because
Nobody Was Watching.

LinkedIn Hiring Assistant (LIHA) is LinkedIn's AI-powered recruiting product – a flagship commercial offering where product adoption directly drives renewal revenue. When customers don't assign seats, enable the product, or send InMails within the first few weeks, they churn. But with hundreds of accounts per rep, no one could manually track who was falling behind. I built the system that does it automatically.

Problem: ADs, AEs, and CSMs had no visibility into which LIHA contracts were below adoption thresholds – they found out at renewal when it was too late
What I built: End-to-end automated pipeline that identifies underperforming contracts, generates personalized HTML alerts per rep, and delivers ~2,000 emails every Thursday
Result: 35% of flagged contracts resolved low seat assignment within 2 weeks. 22% rep engagement rate on email CTA.

// Step 2 · Data Integrity – Improving Accuracy

Raw Salesforce Data Has Real Problems. Here's How I Fix Them.

1
Competitor Accuracy
Competitor is required at Stage 3 via the CI_Opportunity_Competitor junction (908-record catalog). The issue isn't missing data – it's accuracy. Reps pick the wrong competitor or default to the obvious name. One deal can have multiple competitors; the Leading flag tracks who actually won.
→ Validate against CI_Competitor catalog. Build on the existing zero-shot classification to improve entity resolution. Flag deals where Leading competitor changed post-close. Cross-check with Competitive_Notes text.
2
Duplicate Deal Records
Each deal has a unique Opportunity ID. But duplicates appear from: (a) field changes firing multiple events – rep updates Competitor 10 min after close, creating a second record; (b) same deal in both batch export and real-time stream. If 30 of 108 losses are dupes, the scorecard is wrong.
→ Group by Opportunity ID, keep latest timestamp, discard the rest.
3
Deriving Usage from Asset Hierarchy
Usage isn't a stored field – it's derived from Account → Asset Line Items (Apttus) → child usage objects. Need to build a composite metric (license util + active users + feature adoption) and validate for accounts where tracking isn't configured or assets are missing.
→ Build the derivation as a transform step. Range-validate the composite output. NULL accounts with no asset data – flag in quality log.
4
Cross-System ID Resolution
Salesforce IDs are consistent within the CRM (15/18 char), but when tables from different source systems land in Snowflake, the same customer can appear with different identifiers. Joins fail silently.
→ Standardize IDs at the Snowflake layer before any joins. Map external IDs to Salesforce Account_ID as the canonical key.

// Step 3 · Join – The Most Important Design Decision

Connect the Deal to Usage at the Right Point in Time.

Why does timing matter? A customer churned 3 months ago. Their usage today is 0%. But on the day the deal closed, their usage was 18%. If we join to today, every churned account looks the same – we learn nothing. We need usage as of the close date, stored as a daily snapshot.
Decision 1 – Critical
Point-in-Time, Not Current State
✗ Wrong approach
Join to current usage. Every churned customer shows 0% – tells you nothing.
✓ Right approach
Join to usage as of close date. Daily snapshots stored via SCD Type 2. Captures real signal.
Nexus Corp – deal closed Nov 15. Usage on Nov 15: 18%. Usage today: 0% (they churned). Joining to today's data would make every churned account look the same.
Decision 2 – Important
LEFT JOIN, Not INNER JOIN
INNER JOIN
Drops deals with no usage data. Biases analysis toward accounts we already monitored.
LEFT JOIN
Keeps ALL losses. NULL usage = tracking not configured = itself a risk signal.
Surface blind spots, not just the accounts you were already watching.
Result – One Row per Deal-Competitor (OPP-0847 / Nexus Corp) One deal may have 2–3 competitors. The Leading flag identifies who won.
Leading: Rival Analytics Co. $84,000 Close: Nov 15 + Usage @ Close: 18% MAU: 3 of 50 Inactive: 74 days = Tier: LOW Risk Flag: TRUE Trend: ↘ declining

// Why 12 Months Matters – The Trend Tells What a Snapshot Can't

Same Endpoint. Three Different Problems.
Three Different Responses.

A usage score of 18% at close means nothing without context. Was it always 18%? Did it used to be 70%? Did it drop in the last 60 days? The shape of the decline tells you what to do.

Pattern A – Predictable Decline
70% → 18% over 12 months
Gradual disengagement. The warning was there for months. This loss was preventable.
Response: CS should have been alerted at month 6. Proactive intervention window was wide open.
Pattern B – Never Adopted
~20% the entire time
Never got off the ground. Onboarding failure, not churn. Different root cause entirely.
Response: This is an onboarding problem. Fix the implementation process, not the retention process.
Pattern C – Sudden Drop
65% → 10% in 2 months
Something specific happened. Champion left. Competitor demo. Support failure. Investigate immediately.
Response: Pull the support tickets. Check if the champion is still at the company. This one has a specific cause.
Implementation: Instead of joining to one snapshot at close, we pull a time series – monthly usage for the 12 months before close_date. This requires daily usage snapshots (SCD Type 2). The sparkline per account in the dashboard makes the pattern visible at a glance.

// The Most Nuanced Quadrant – This Is Where L7 Thinking Matters

24% of Losses Break the Pattern.
These Need Different Thinking.

✓ In Our Control – We Can Fix This
Price undercut
Pricing Team
Competitor offered 30% less. Customer was happy with the product but the economics didn't work. If this is concentrated in Mid-Market, the pricing tier may be wrong for that segment.
Feature parity crossed
Product Team
Competitor shipped what was our differentiator. Now they're equal on capability and cheaper. This is a roadmap input – not a dashboard metric.
Relationship failure
Sales Ops
Champion left the account. New decision-maker had a prior relationship with the competitor. CSM assignment model needs review.
✗ Not In Our Control – Track & Learn
Vendor consolidation
CIO standardizing on Oracle/SAP. Product was fine. Politics won. Nothing we could have done – but we should track frequency by account size and region.
What I'd Build
Cross-reference loss reason classification from the Enrich step with this quadrant specifically. Surface: "Of the 24% of losses where usage was high, what are the top 3 loss reasons by ARR?" – The answer becomes a product brief, a pricing review, and a CSM model change. Three teams. Three fixes. Not just a number on a dashboard. And this is exactly the battlecard feedback loop – classified loss reasons flow back to update battlecard content, with quarterly sign-off to keep it fresh.
